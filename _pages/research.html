I study how manipulation tasks are specified to robots and how they are represented inside them, and what each choice assumes. A task can be given as a goal, a reward, a language instruction, or a set of demonstrations. Inside the robot it can be represented explicitly, as constraints on how the object should move, or implicitly, through what a learned model picked up from its training data. These choices decide what a method can do: which tasks it can be specified for, what it means for it to generalize, and how anyone can tell the task is done.

One representation, studied in depth

Most of my PhD explored one explicit representation: describing a task by the motion the object goes through, as a constant screw motion or a sequence of them. By Chasles' theorem, any rigid body motion can be approximated arbitrarily closely by such a sequence, so the same description covers opening a drawer, turning a knob, pivoting a box, pouring and scooping. I used it to judge whether a grasp can produce the motion, to find grasps from point clouds, and to plan the robot's motion, often from a single kinesthetic demonstration.

  1. 1 Describe the task as a sequence of constant screw motions of the object
  2. 2 Score grasps by whether they can produce that motion
  3. 3 Find grasps from point clouds, and regrasp when one grasp isn't enough
  4. 4 Plan the motion with screw linear interpolation, from demonstrations and with contacts
