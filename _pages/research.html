I study how manipulation tasks are specified to robots and how they are represented, and what each choice assumes. For example, a task can be given during training, as a reward or a dataset of demonstrations, or at deployment, as a goal, a language instruction, or a single demonstration. Tasks can be represented explicitly, as mathematical constraints on how the object should move, or implicitly, through what a learned model captured from its training data. These choices decide what a method can do: which tasks it can be specified for, what it means for it to generalize, and how we can quantify if a task is successful.

Utilizing the inherent geometric structure of motion for representing tasks

Most of my PhD explored one explicit representation: describing a task in terms of the motion the object goes through, as a constant screw motion or a sequence of them. By Chasles' theorem, any rigid body motion can be approximated arbitrarily closely as a sequence of constant screw motions, so the same description covers opening a drawer, turning a knob, pivoting a box, pouring, scooping or even transplanting saplings in a containerized vertical farm. Most robotic manipulation today remains limited to pick-and-place operations, in which grasp synthesis and motion planning for manipulation are traditionally treated as isolated problems. On the contrary, grasping and motion planning are closely related. The manner in which an object is grasped should be determined by how it is supposed to be moved. While pivoting a box, for example, the grasping region depends on the axis of pivot, and good grasps for one pivoting axis may be bad for another. Utilizing the screw-geometric structure of motion as the task representation, I developed algorithmic and learning-based approaches for task-oriented grasp synthesis from sensor data. The same representation can also be used to plan the motion of the object using Screw Linear Interpolation (ScLERP) with Resolved Motion Rate Control (RMRC). Moreover, if the sequence of screws, corresponding to the desired object's motion is not know beforehand, then it can be extracted from a single kinesthetic demonstration and used to plan the motion.

  1. 1 Describe the task as a sequence of constant screw motions of the object
  2. 2 Score grasps by whether they can produce that motion
  3. 3 Find grasps from point clouds, and regrasp when one grasp isn't enough
  4. 4 Plan the motion with screw linear interpolation, from demonstrations and with contacts
